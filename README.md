# pendulum_clock

on wall clock with pendulum what is the part that flexes as it swings

The part that flexes as a clock pendulum swings is the suspension spring. 

It is a short, thin strip of spring steel at the top of the pendulum assembly. Instead of grinding against a pivot bearing, the suspension spring bends slightly back and forth with each swing to reduce friction and allow smooth motion. 

• How it connects: The suspension spring anchors to the clock movement at the top and holds the pendulum leader (often via a small hook or t-hook), which in turn carries the pendulum rod and bob.

• Why it matters: Because it is very thin metal, bending or tearing the suspension spring can throw off the swing or stop the clock from running. 
(Note: Battery-operated quartz pendulum clocks typically use a motorized swing arm or magnet rather than a flexing suspension spring.) 

You can read more about how these pieces fit together in the Clockworks Helpdesk guide or Gift of Time's wall clock pendulum tips. 

To repair a mechanical clock pendulum that has stopped swinging or has a broken suspension spring, replacing the suspension spring is almost always the cleanest, easiest fix rather than trying to bend or straighten a kinked one.
This straightforward swap takes about 15 to 30 minutes and requires minimal mechanical skill.

What You'll Need

• Tools: Small needle-nose pliers, a small flathead screwdriver (if a retaining pin or screw holds the spring), and a digital caliper or ruler.
• Materials: Replacement suspension spring (matched by length, width, and mounting hole/slot style—available from suppliers like Clockworks or Timesavers).

How to Replace a Broken Suspension Spring

1. Unhook the pendulum assembly. Lift the pendulum bob and rod slightly upward to unhook it from the leader or suspension spring hook inside the clock case, then set it safely aside.
2. Remove the old suspension spring. Look at the top of the clock movement where the pendulum hangs. Depending on your clock's make (such as Hermle, Kieninger, or Seth Thomas), either slide the spring out of its slot, remove a small brass retaining pin, or loosen a tiny mounting screw.
3. Measure for an exact match. Lay the old spring flat (even if broken into two pieces) and measure its total length, width, and the distance between the mounting holes or pins so you order the exact replacement.
4. Install the new spring. Slide the top end of the new suspension spring into the movement's bracket and secure it with the pin or screw. Make sure the thin metal blade sits straight and is not twisted.
5. Rehang the pendulum through the crutch. Guide the pendulum rod up through the loop of the crutch (the small wire fork connected to the clock's escapement) and hook the top of the rod onto the bottom of the new suspension spring.
6. Level the clock and put it "in beat." Hang the clock back on the wall and listen to the tick-tock. Tilt the bottom of the clock case slightly left or right until the "tick" and "tock" sound completely even, then adjust the nut at the bottom of the pendulum bob to regulate the time.
Warning: If your clock is key-wound or weight-driven and needs internal gear repairs beyond the pendulum hanger, do not remove the movement plates yourself unless you have a mainspring let-down tool—wound clock mainsprings store enough tension to cause serious hand injuries if released suddenly.
